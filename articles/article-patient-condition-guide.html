Patient Condition & Diagnosis Guide — Every Illness and Treatment in Animal Hospital Anomaly (2026)

Every patient condition matched with its cure — visual diagnosis cues, item-to-condition mapping, room assignments, and the anomalous conditions that cost sanity if misdiagnosed. The complete diagnostic reference you will keep open on your second monitor.

You walk into the treatment room. The patient is on the bed. The HUD indicator flashes a condition icon. Now what? You have got three seconds to identify the illness, find the matching item, and administer treatment before the queue backs up, emergencies fire, and your sanity starts to bleed. Every experienced player has been here — the clock is ticking, the patient is flashing red, and you are staring at the HUD trying to remember whether Cooling Gel treats High Fever or whether that is Medicine.

This Animal Hospital Anomaly patient condition guide is the diagnostic reference you wish the game gave you on Shift 1. We cover every normal condition currently in the game with its visual diagnosis cue, the matching treatment item, the room where treatment completes fastest, and the recovery timing you can expect. We also cover the anomalous conditions — the illnesses that look normal but have wrong cure items attached, and the misdiagnosis penalty that punishes players who treat without verifying. Whether you are an Intern running your first shift or a Surgeon pushing Shift 5 with five simultaneous emergencies, this guide will sharpen your diagnostic eye and save you from the treatment mistakes that kill runs.

How Patient Conditions Work in Animal Hospital Anomaly

Every patient who passes the anomaly check — meaning you verified them as human across all three detection layers — has exactly one condition to treat. The condition is visible on the patient's HUD indicator as a small icon above their head. Click the patient or walk close enough to scan them and the condition name appears in the interaction panel. The game does not force you to memorize visual cues for diagnosis — the HUD tells you the condition directly. But reading the HUD costs a second, and in later shifts that second is the difference between a clean run and a cascade failure.

The real diagnostic challenge is not identifying the condition — it is verifying that the condition is correct. Some anomalies manipulate the HUD display, showing a normal condition like "Flu" while the patient's actual state requires a different cure. Others present conditions that do not exist in the standard database. This is the anomalous condition layer and it is the primary way the game punishes players who treat blindly based on HUD text alone. Always cross-reference the visual cue with the condition name before administering treatment.

Diagnosis Speed: Reading Visual Cues vs. HUD Text

Experienced players diagnose by visual cue rather than reading the HUD text. The HUD text requires you to aim at the patient, wait for the interaction prompt, and read a line of text. Visual diagnosis is instantaneous — you see the blood stain, you grab Bandages, you treat. The speed difference per patient is roughly 1-2 seconds. Over a full 30-patient shift, that is 30-60 seconds saved. In Shift 5 where the queue never stops, 60 seconds is the difference between staying ahead and falling behind.

However, visual diagnosis alone is insufficient for anomalous conditions. Anomalies can manipulate visual cues — a patient who looks like they have Bleeding might actually be in a corrupted state that requires a different cure. The rule is simple: diagnose fast by visual cue, but verify the HUD text matches the visual cue before treating. If the HUD says "Bleeding" and the patient is visibly bleeding, treat immediately. If the HUD says "Bleeding" but the patient has no visible blood stain, pause and run the triple-layer detection check — you may be looking at an anomalous condition.

Anomalous Conditions: The Misdiagnosis Trap

Anomalous conditions are the game's way of punishing players who treat without verifying. They appear in two forms:

Type 1: HUD-Lie Anomalies

The HUD displays a normal condition name — "Flu" for example — but the patient's visual cues do not match. A patient who has the "Flu" icon but shows no coughing, no nasal drip, and instead has a perfectly still, emotionless face is not a Flu patient. They are an anomalous entity disguised as a standard patient. Treating them with Curses Syrup will fail — the treatment will not apply, the patient will not recover, and you will lose 10-15 sanity points for the failed interaction.

The counter is always the same: trust visual cues over HUD text. If the visual cue and the HUD text disagree, run the triple-layer detection check. If the patient is anomalous, reject them at the door. Do not attempt treatment on a patient whose condition cannot be verified.

Type 2: Non-Database Conditions

Some patients display a condition icon that does not match any entry in the standard condition database. Current examples include Curse (dark purple HUD icon), Possession (red-black HUD icon), and Corruption (static-filled HUD icon). These conditions have no standard treatment item. Attempting to treat them with any normal item will fail and cost sanity.

Non-database conditions are handled through the anomaly rejection system, not the treatment system. If you see a condition icon you do not recognize, reject the patient immediately. Do not waste time searching for a cure that does not exist. See our Anomaly Detection Flowchart for the complete verification process for unrecognized conditions.

Item-to-Condition Mapping: The Quick Reference

Sometimes you pick up an item first and want to know which condition it treats. Here is the reverse mapping from item to condition:

  • Bandages → Bleeding
  • Cough Syrup → Flu
  • Cooling Gel → High Fever
  • Eye Drops → Dried Eyes
  • Herbs → Stomach Ache
  • IV Bag → Dehydration
  • Maple Syrup → Low Sugar
  • Medicine → Headache
  • Medkit → Bruises
  • Ointment → Rashes

This mapping is worth memorizing. It takes approximately 10 minutes of Shift 1 repetition to commit all ten pairs to memory. Once memorized, you will never again hesitate in a treatment room wondering which item to use. The investment pays for itself within a single shift.

In Shift 5, where IV Room demand peaks, prioritize Dehydration patients for the IV Room above all other conditions except active emergencies.

Multi-Condition Priority: When Patients Stack Up

Starting in Shift 3, patients begin appearing with conditions that require multi-step treatment chains. The most common examples:

  • Dehydration + Bruises — Treat Dehydration first in the IV Room, then treat Bruises in Room 3. Total time: ~19 seconds.
  • Bleeding + Rashes — Both treat in Room 2. Apply Bandages first, then Ointment. Total time: ~14 seconds.
  • Flu + Low Sugar — Treat Flu in Room 6 first (contagious), then Low Sugar in Room 1. Total time: ~16 seconds.

The general rule for multi-condition patients: treat the more severe condition first. Severity order from most to least urgent is: Dehydration → Bleeding → High Fever → Flu → Bruises → Low Sugar → Rashes → Headache → Stomach Ache → Dried Eyes. This ordering minimizes the chance of the patient's condition worsening while you treat the secondary issue.

The Misdiagnosis Penalty: What Happens When You Treat Wrong

Administering the wrong treatment item to a patient triggers the misdiagnosis penalty. The consequences escalate with severity:

  • Wrong item, standard condition — The treatment fails. The item is consumed. The patient does not recover. 5-second cooldown before you can try again. No sanity loss.
  • Wrong item, anomalous condition — The treatment fails. The item is consumed. The patient does not recover. 10-15 sanity loss plus a 5-second cooldown.
  • Repeated wrong items on same patient — After three failed treatment attempts, the patient enters a critical state — their condition timer drops to 30 seconds. If you fail to treat within 30 seconds, the patient codes and you lose 20 sanity points.

The key insight: a wrong guess is expensive but not fatal. The real danger is repeated wrong guesses on the same patient. If you treat once and the item does not apply, stop. Re-evaluate. Check the visual cue again. Read the HUD text carefully. Run the detection check if necessary. Do not spam items hoping one will stick — the third failed attempt triggers the critical state timer and you are on the clock.

Advanced: Condition-Item Synergies for Speedrunners

Speedrunners exploit condition-item routing to minimize movement time. The hospital layout means certain item-condition combinations require less walking than others if you plan your route. Key optimizations:

The Room 2 Double-Stop

Bandages and Ointment both spawn outside Room 2 and both treat in Room 2. If you see both a Bleeding patient and a Rashes patient in the queue, grab one of each item and treat both patients in a single room visit. Saves ~8 seconds of hallway walking.

The IV Room Priority Pass

Dehydration patients can only be treated in the IV Room, and the IV Room is centrally located. Speedrunners treat Dehydration patients first whenever the IV Room is free, then use the IV Room's central position as a pivot point to reach any other room in the hospital in under 4 seconds.

The Supply Room Restock Loop

Items respawn on shelves every 20 seconds. If you are running a high-volume shift, establish a restock loop that passes each shelf every 40 seconds. Grab two of each item per pass. This keeps your inventory stocked without dedicated shopping trips. The optimal loop starts at Room 1, proceeds clockwise through Rooms 2-8, and ends at the IV Room.

Pro Tip: The Hospital Secretary NPC in the lobby will call out the next patient's condition over the PA system if you stand near the front desk for 3 seconds. This audio cue lets you identify the condition before you even enter the treatment room, giving you a 3-second head start to grab the correct item. Use this on Shift 1-2 while you are still memorizing the condition-item mapping. By Shift 3 you should no longer need it.

Condition Confusion: Similar Visual Cues to Watch For

Some conditions share visual elements that confuse newer players. Here are the most common mix-ups and how to tell them apart:

High Fever vs. Headache

Both cause the patient to hold or touch their head. The differentiator is the sweating animation. High Fever patients visibly sweat — small particle effects around the forehead. Headache patients do not sweat. If the patient has a red forehead and is sweating, it is High Fever → Cooling Gel. If the patient has a neutral forehead and no sweat, it is Headache → Medicine.

Bleeding vs. Rashes

Both present as red marks on the skin. The differentiator is texture and location. Bleeding shows as dark red patches concentrated on limbs and torso, with animated dripping. Rashes show as small red dots spread evenly across exposed skin, with no animation. Bleeding → Bandages. Rashes → Ointment.

Flu vs. Stomach Ache

Both cause the patient to hold their upper body. The differentiator is body area. Flu patients hold their chest/throat and cough. Stomach Ache patients hold their midsection and hunch forward. Flu → Cough Syrup. Stomach Ache → Herbs.

Low Sugar vs. Dehydration

Both cause movement impairment. The differentifier is skin appearance. Low Sugar patients are pale/white-skinned with a dizzy stumble walk cycle. Dehydration patients have normal skin tone but sunken eyes and cracked lips. Low Sugar → Maple Syrup. Dehydration → IV Bag.

Item Availability by Shift

Not all treatment items are available from Shift 1. The item pool unlocks progressively:

  • Shift 1: Bandages, Medkit, Eye Drops, Medicine, Maple Syrup, Ointment
  • Shift 2: All of the above + Cough Syrup, Herbs
  • Shift 3: All of the above + IV Bag, Cooling Gel
  • Shift 4-5: All 10 standard treatment items available

If you encounter a Dehydration or High Fever patient before Shift 3, the treatment item has not yet unlocked. These patients appear rarely in early shifts — roughly 2-3% of the patient pool — but when they do, you have two options: reject them as anomalous (safe but loses potential coin reward) or skip them and wait for the item to unlock (if you are near the shift end). In competitive play, reject is almost always the right call.

The Condition-Item Memory Drill

If you want to memorize the condition-item mapping as fast as possible, run this drill on Shift 1:

  1. Open this guide on a second screen or your phone.
  2. Play Shift 1 with the explicit goal of treating all 10 condition types.
  3. For each patient, say the condition name and item name out loud before treating.
  4. After Shift 1, play Shift 2 without looking at the guide.
  5. For every hesitation, note the condition-item pair you forgot.
  6. Review the missed pairs. Play Shift 2 again. Repeat until zero hesitations.

Most players complete this drill in 3-4 shifts — roughly 45 minutes of play. Once the mapping is automatic, you free up cognitive bandwidth for anomaly detection and emergency response. The players who struggle past Shift 4 are almost always the ones who still think about which item treats which condition instead of doing it instinctively.

Common Diagnostic Mistakes That Cost Runs

Here are the most frequent condition-related mistakes that end runs at every skill level:

  • Treating HUD text without visual verification — HUD-Lie anomalies exploit this habit. Always cross-check the visual cue.
  • Guessing the item when unsure — One wrong guess costs 5 seconds and an item. Two wrong guesses cost 15 seconds and two items. Three wrong guesses trigger the critical state. When in doubt, re-examine the patient before treating.
  • Ignoring room optimization — Treating a patient in the wrong room adds 2-3 seconds. Over 30 patients, that is 60-90 seconds of wasted time. Always route to the correct room.
  • Carrying only one item type — If you grab five Bandages and the queue is full of Flu patients, you are running back to shelves constantly. Carry 2-3 different item types depending on the current queue composition.
  • Treating anomalous conditions as rare standard conditions — If you see a condition icon you do not recognize, reject. Do not search for a cure. Treatments that do not exist cannot be discovered by trying every item in your inventory.

Summary: The Diagnostic Checklist

Before you administer any treatment, run through this 3-second diagnostic checklist:

  1. Identify the condition — Read the HUD text OR recognize the visual cue (experienced players use visual, beginners use HUD).
  2. Cross-verify — Does the visual cue match the HUD text? If yes, proceed. If no, run the triple-layer detection check.
  3. Verify the item — Do you have the correct treatment item? If not, grab it from the nearest shelf.
  4. Route to the correct room — Move the patient to the room optimized for their condition.
  5. Administer and monitor — Apply the item. Watch the recovery gauge. Discharge when complete.

This checklist is identical to the 6-Step Patient Workflow but with the diagnostic layer made explicit. The difference between players who clear Shift 5 and players who crash at Shift 3 comes down to this: experienced players diagnose in under 3 seconds and never treat a patient whose condition they have not verified. Beginners read the HUD, grab an item, treat the patient, and hope for the best. Hope is not a strategy. Diagnosis is.
